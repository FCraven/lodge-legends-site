Our Standard

Documented, not folklore

If it can't be sourced, it isn't posted.

The internet is full of "famous Freemasons" who never were — Bach, Lincoln, Beethoven, Einstein all make the viral lists, and none has a documented lodge record. This project holds a harder line: every membership claim traces to Grand Lodge registries, lodge minutes, or reputable Masonic scholarship, and every image is public-domain or properly licensed with its source recorded.

Brethren who know the history will read these posts. They're written to survive that audience.

Take Part

Where the brethren come in

Thursdays are ours

The "This Lodge" series runs on what we give it — a photo from a charity drive, the hall, a degree night's fellowship (always with consent, always within what the lodge is comfortable sharing). If you have something worth showing, pass it to the officers running the page.

Review before it airs

Every prepared post sits in a preview queue about two weeks ahead of its date. Interested members can look over what's coming, suggest corrections, or flag anything — nothing publishes that the lodge wouldn't stand behind.

Follow, share, comment

The simplest help is the biggest: follow the page, share the posts you like, and answer thoughtfully when friends ask about them. Every post is a door someone might knock on.

Know the history?

If you catch an error or know a source we missed, say so — corrections make the project stronger, and that's the point of the standard we've set.

Fair Questions

What members have asked

Does this expose anything it shouldn't?

No. Everything published is drawn from openly available history and scholarship — the same material in Grand Lodge museums and public libraries. Anything touching ceremony is reviewed by the lodge before it airs, and the modes of recognition are never part of this project.

Who runs it?

The research, writing, and scheduling run on an automated pipeline built for the lodge; the page itself is administered by our own members, who approve the queue and handle every reply. Machines do the clerical work — the lodge keeps the voice.

Why do this at all?

Because good men can't petition a lodge they never think about. Telling our story well — honestly, publicly, and with some craft — is how the next generation finds us.
